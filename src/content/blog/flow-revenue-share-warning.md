Flow revenue share is the metric people quote when they want to sound like the email is working. Sixty percent from flows. Automated. Running while you sleep.

I looked at an account doing exactly that. Flows were producing well over half the email revenue, which is the number you would put in a deck. Total revenue was down thirty percent year on year.

Both things were true, and the first one was a symptom of the second.

## The Same Number, Two Opposite Meanings

A ratio has two sides, and a ratio going up tells you nothing about which side moved.

Flow revenue share rises when your flows get better. It also rises when everything else gets worse. Campaigns underperform, traffic softens, the list stops growing, and the flows keep quietly converting the people who were coming anyway. The share climbs. The business shrinks.

On a dashboard those are indistinguishable. Same chart, same direction, opposite diagnosis, opposite response.

Which is why the number is close to useless on its own. It only means something next to the absolute figures, and almost nobody looks at both.

## Why Flows Flatter Themselves

There is a second thing inflating that share, and it is structural rather than accidental.

Flows fire at people who have already done something. Added to cart, browsed, bought last month, joined the list. Every one of those is a person who has demonstrated intent, which is exactly the population most likely to convert with or without an email.

Campaigns go to everyone else. People who were not thinking about you today.

So the flow half of the ratio is measured against a warm audience and the campaign half against a cold one. That is not a fair fight, and it is [the same over-crediting problem that makes abandoned cart look better than it is](/blog/abandoned-cart-flow/). Flow revenue is real revenue. A meaningful slice of it was not caused by the flow.

## What the Account Actually Had Wrong

The flows were not the problem, and neither was the flow share. It was the thing the flow share was hiding.

Nobody was being told apart. There was no difference in how the account treated someone who had bought four times and someone who had never bought at all. Every campaign went to broadly the same audience with broadly the same message.

So the flows carried the account, because the flows were the only thing responding to what a person had actually done. Everything else was one message to one list. The high flow share was not automation working. It was the rest of the program not doing anything a flow was not already doing.

Fixing that is not a flow project. It starts with [grouping people by how recently and how often they buy](/blog/klaviyo-rfm-segmentation/), so that campaigns have someone specific to talk to, and then giving those groups genuinely different things to read.

## How to Tell Which One You Have

Three checks, in order.

**Look at the absolute numbers, not the split.** Is campaign revenue flat, growing or falling in real terms. A rising flow share with rising campaign revenue is a healthy account. A rising flow share with falling campaign revenue is a warning that has been reported to you as a success.

**Check how many campaigns went to a defined audience.** Not engaged-minus-suppressions, which is a deliverability decision. An audience chosen because the message suited them specifically. If the answer over a month is none, the flow share is telling you about the absence of campaigns rather than the quality of flows.

**Check what is feeding the top.** Flows convert demand. They do not create it. If list growth has stalled, the flows are working a shrinking pool, and the share will keep rising right up until it stops mattering.

## What a Good Number Looks Like

There isn't one, and be suspicious of anyone who gives you one.

The healthy version is boring: both halves growing in absolute terms, the ratio wandering around and nobody caring much. The unhealthy version is a ratio moving decisively in one direction while the totals move in the other.

Which means flow revenue share is not a performance metric at all. It is a diagnostic, and only when you read it alongside the numbers it is a share of.
